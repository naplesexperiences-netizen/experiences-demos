On every €800 booking, Booking.com keeps €144. It is not a one-off fee: it is the 18% that leaves every time a guest clicks "Book" on a portal instead of on your own site. Across 200 bookings a year that is €28,800 handed to an intermediary who, in the meantime, also keeps your customer's email address. The question is no longer whether to reduce your dependence on them, but how much you are losing every month you put the decision off.

What an OTA booking really costs — and it is not just the commission

When a hotel manager works out the cost of Booking.com or Expedia, they stop at the contract percentage: 15%, 17%, 18% if you are in a visibility programme. That is the number on the invoice, so it is the only one that reaches the budget. And it is the least painful part of the bill.

1. The commission is the tip of the iceberg

Layered on top of the declared 18% are invisible costs: the preferred partner programme that adds three to five percentage points to stay visible, sponsored campaigns inside the portal, cancellation penalties handled under the OTA's rules rather than yours. Many Campania properties we analyse pay a real distribution cost of 21–24%, not the 15% they believe.

2. The customer is not yours, it is theirs

Every OTA booking arrives with a masked email address (@guest.booking.com) that stops working a few weeks after check-out. No real phone number, no marketing consent, no preferences. You have hosted someone for four nights and at the end of the stay you cannot write to them at all: that customer lives in a database in Amsterdam, not in yours.

3. No remarketing means no recurring return

A guest acquired directly enters a cycle that continues: a pre-stay email for upselling, a review request, an offer when the season reopens, a loyalty code. A guest acquired through an OTA reaches a dead end. If they return, they return through Booking, and you pay commission a second time on a customer you had already won.

The billboard paradox: OTAs are useful, just not as your only channel

Something many consultants avoid saying: Booking.com is not the enemy. It is the largest engine of travel discovery in the world, and for a 30-room property in Positano or on Ischia it is a shop window no marketing budget could replicate.

The phenomenon is called the billboard effect: the traveller discovers the property on a portal, then searches the hotel's name on Google and decides elsewhere. Serious estimates put it at 45–55% of users who, having seen a property on an OTA, visit the official site before booking. If that site is slow, if the booking engine takes five steps, if the price is identical to the portal's, the user goes back to the OTA and you pay commission on a customer who had already been delivered to you.

The aim is not to eliminate the portals: it is to shift the balance. A healthy mix for an independent property in Campania is 60% direct and owned channels, 40% OTAs and intermediaries. Those who reach 100% direct almost always lose volume and occupancy; those who stay below 15% direct are financing a competitor's growth.

The six pillars of a book-direct strategy that works

1. A fast, mobile-first booking engine: fewer than three clicks

Sixty-eight per cent of traffic to Campania hotel sites comes from phones, yet mobile conversion averages a third of desktop. The reason is almost always the same: booking engines designed in 2016, with calendars that do not open properly on touch and forms with seven fields. The working rule is blunt but effective: from the home page to the payment page there should be at most three clicks, and the whole thing should load in under three seconds on 4G.

2. A visible best rate guarantee, not one buried in the footer

If the guest does not know your site has the best rate, they will go and check on Booking and stay there. The "best price guaranteed" badge belongs above the fold, next to the booking button, worded plainly: "Book here for the lowest price. Find it cheaper elsewhere and we will refund the difference." It costs nothing and moves conversion by 8 to 14 percentage points.

3. Exclusive perks on direct: change the game, not the price

Competing on price with an OTA is a war you lose. Competing on perceived value is one you win at almost no cost. Late check-out, an upgrade, breakfast included, a discounted transfer from the port: these cost you a few euros and the guest values them at tens.

4. Google Hotel Ads and metasearch: catch the people already looking for you

When someone searches your hotel's name on Google, the side panel shows the portals' rates. If you are not in that panel with your own rate, you are handing Booking the click on your own brand. Google Hotel Ads works on commission — typically 10–12%, often less on a cost-per-click basis — and remains considerably cheaper than an OTA, with the advantage that the customer lands on your booking engine and the data stays yours.

5. Email marketing to your own database

A list of 5,000 profiled contacts is worth, for a 40-room property, between €40,000 and €70,000 of direct revenue a year. It needs minimal segmentation — past guests, people who only enquired, high-season against low-season stayers — and three well-made campaigns a year: season opening, a targeted last-minute offer, a holiday promotion.

6. A light loyalty scheme, not a points card

Forget complicated systems. What works far better is one simple rule you can state in a line: "Anyone who has stayed with us always books at 10% off on the official site, for life." It costs a tenth of the margin but turns an occasional customer into a recurring one, and above all it is the lever that moves the rebooking from the portal to your own channel.

Rate parity: what the contract really says, and what Italian law says

This is every hotelier's first fear: "If I put a lower price on my own site, Booking will penalise me or close my account." It is a widespread belief and, in Italy, legally unfounded.

📌 In Italy, strict parity clauses have been void since 2017
Italy's annual competition law (Law 124/2017, article 1, paragraph 166) declared null any contractual clause preventing an accommodation business from offering better prices and booking conditions on its own direct channels than on intermediation platforms. In practice: you may legally sell for less on your own site. The EU Platform-to-Business Regulation and the Digital Markets Act have reinforced the same direction.

That said, undercutting aggressively and publicly is rarely the best strategy, because it triggers an algorithmic reaction in the portal's ranking. The intelligent route is the one the chains have used for years: matched public rate, differentiated value. The same visible price, but on direct you add breakfast, late check-out, a more flexible cancellation. And for closed rates — promotional codes, newsletter-only rates, offers for past guests — you have complete freedom, because those are not public prices.

The first 90 days

Days 1–30: measure, and fix the foundations

Before spending a euro on advertising you need to know where you stand. Calculate the real distribution cost channel by channel, measure site speed on mobile with PageSpeed Insights, count how many clicks it genuinely takes to book. Install event tracking on the booking engine: without data on where people drop out, every later optimisation is guesswork. In parallel, put the best rate guarantee and a starting price on the home page — two changes that take hours and produce results immediately.

Days 31–60: make the direct channel competitive

This is conversion work. A three-step booking engine, a calendar usable on a phone, multiple languages if you serve international markets. Define and publish the package of exclusive perks, stating it on every room page. Switch on Google Hotel Ads at least for brand queries: it is the fastest way to stop paying commission on people already searching for you by name. Start collecting emails properly, at booking and at check-in.

Days 61–90: build the recurring return

Automate the email cycle: confirmation, pre-stay with upselling, post-stay with a review request and a loyalty code. Switch on abandoned-cart recovery. Launch the loyalty scheme to the database you already have, however small. From here it is monthly maintenance: watch the channel mix, test the perks, move budget from OTA inventory towards direct without sudden movements.

⚠️ Do not cut the OTAs abruptly
The most expensive mistake we see is the hotelier who, encouraged by early results, drastically reduces portal inventory or closes a channel overnight. The outcome is almost always the same: the billboard effect collapses, you vanish from discovery searches, occupancy falls 10 to 15 points, and it takes two seasons to recover the lost ranking. Reducing intermediation is a progression, not a switch. Cut your dependence by growing direct, not by cutting indirect.
