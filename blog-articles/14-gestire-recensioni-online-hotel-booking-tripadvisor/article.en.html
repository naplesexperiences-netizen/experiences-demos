Every tenth of a point on your Booking score is worth an average of 2.3% of RevPAR. Going from 8.1 to 9.0 is nine tenths: roughly 20% more revenue from the same rooms in the same season. No advertising spend returns as much. Yet 71% of Italian properties never reply to negative reviews, and let the unhappy guest write the only version of the story the next traveller will read.

Reputation is not an image question: it is a pricing lever. The score decides where you appear, whether you survive the filter the user applies before even looking at the photographs, and how much you can charge for the same room.

Why the score matters more than you think

Three separate mechanisms stack up, and almost no hotelier keeps them apart.

1. The ranking algorithm treats it as a strong signal

Booking.com orders results by combining commission, conversion, availability, rate and quality. The score enters twice: as an absolute value and as freshness — how many reviews you have had in the past twelve months. Two identical properties at the same price do not appear in the same position if one has 8.2 from 30 reviews and the other 8.9 from 210.

2. The "8+" filter cuts out anyone below it

Sixty-two per cent of users set a minimum score before scrolling, and almost always the value they pick is 8+ ("Very good"). Below that threshold you are not competing badly: you are not appearing. The next threshold, decisive in high-spending northern European markets, is 9+.

3. The score is the price you can afford to charge

This is the point that matters most. At the same category and location, a guest will accept 10–15% more if your score is visibly better than your competitors'. Reputation is what lets you leave the price war without losing occupancy.

The volume rule: 200 reviews at 8.7 beat 40 at 9.2

This is the most counter-intuitive idea in reputation management, and the one that loses the most money. A high score built on few judgements is worth less than a slightly lower one built on many.

  • Perceived trust. A user mentally discounts a 9.2 from 40 reviews: they know four friends and a relative would do it. An 8.7 from 200 is statistically credible.
  • Stability. With 40 reviews, a single score of 4 drags you down 0.13 points; with 200 the same review weighs 0.03. Volume is your insurance against the impossible guest.
  • Algorithmic freshness. The platforms weight recent reviews more heavily. Collecting 15 a month tells the ranking you are alive; sitting at three a year, as far as the algorithm is concerned, you might be closed.

Hence the order of operations: raise the volume first, then work on the average. Doing it the other way round means chasing tenths while the denominator stays too small for anything to show.

Raising the volume without breaking the platforms' rules

The rule you cannot work around: you may not offer anything in exchange for a review, nor pick who to ask based on how happy you think they are. You may ask everyone, at the right moment and on the right channel — and that is where almost everyone goes wrong.

The timing

The useful window is 12 to 36 hours after check-out. Earlier, the guest is travelling; later, the memory blurs and the response rate collapses. Asking during the stay is the classic mistake: it is a judgement on an incomplete experience.

The channel

On Booking the invitation comes from the platform: what you control is reminding the guest at check-out that they will receive it. Ten seconds at the desk doubles the completion rate. On Google and TripAdvisor you generate the request yourself, with a direct link to the review form, never to the profile home page.

QR codes in the room and at the desk

A QR code on a card in the room and one at the desk are the highest-yield channel for Google, and cost twenty euros to print. The wording must be neutral — "Tell us how it went" — never "Leave us five stars", which breaches the platforms' guidelines and can cost you the profile.

The automated post-stay email

It is the only tool that scales. A single email 24 hours after check-out, with the guest's name and one link, takes the review rate from 6–8% to 15–22% of departures. No reminders: whoever does not answer the first will not answer the third.

Replying to a negative review: the five-step formula

One premise changes everything: the reply is not written for the person who complained. It is written for the 300 travellers who will read that exchange while deciding whether to book with you.

  1. Thank them, briefly. One line, no corporate preamble.
  2. Acknowledge the specific fact. Name the problem in their words: the reader has to see that you understood.
  3. Explain without over-apologising. A sentence of context, not a defence. Excessive apology sounds as insincere as a defence.
  4. State a concrete action. What has changed, with a verifiable detail. This is the part that convinces the outside reader.
  5. Move the rest to private. A direct contact, away from the shop window.
❌ The wrong reply ✅ The right reply
"Dear guest, we are very sorry for the inconvenience. Your opinion is extremely important to us. We assure you that our standards are always of the highest level and that the noise you mention is entirely exceptional, not least because no other customer has ever complained. We apologise again and hope to welcome you back soon." "Thank you for telling us, Marta.
The roadworks noise was real, on the mornings of the 12th and 13th, and on the street-side rooms it was heavy.
The works finished on 20 March, and from this week rooms 204–208 are the last to be allocated for short stays.
If you decide to come back, write to me: I'll keep a courtyard-side room for you.
Luca, manager"

Four differences are measurable: the second uses the name, cites verifiable dates, contains a dated action and is signed by a real person. The first denies the problem and closes with a formula already seen on two hundred identical listings.

⚠️ Three mistakes that cost more than the reviews themselves.
Never ask for a review to be removed simply because it is negative: the platforms remove only what breaks their policies — abuse, personal data, false statements, blackmail — and repeated spurious requests flag your profile. Never reply in the heat of the moment: leave twenty-four hours and have a second person read it. Never copy and paste the same reply: anyone reading three identical replies in a row works out in three seconds that nobody read anything, and the damage exceeds that of the original reviews.

The weekly routine: thirty minutes is enough

Reputation is not managed in projects, it is managed by rhythm. A fixed block in the diary, always the same day, returns more than any one-off initiative.

  • 15 minutes — the replies. Every review from the week: negatives first, positives with two lines citing a detail of the stay. None left beyond 48 hours.
  • 10 minutes — the count. On one sheet: Booking score, Google score, new reviews, and the words that recur in the negative texts. Always in the same place.
  • 5 minutes — the briefing. One thing to fix this week, told to whoever has to do it. One, not five: the long list never gets done.
  • Every quarter, 60 minutes — the review. How the six sub-scores are trending, the monthly volume, and a check that your listing and photographs match reality.

💡 Reviews are your free operational roadmap. They are continuous consultancy written by people who paid to use your product. The complaints that repeat are your to-do list, already sorted by how often they appear. If the word "breakfast" comes up eleven times in a negative tone over three months, that is not an opinion: it is a job you are putting off. Extract the recurring themes every quarter and treat them as work orders, not feedback.
