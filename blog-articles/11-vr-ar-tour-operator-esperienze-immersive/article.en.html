VR and AR: the difference in two lines

Before the numbers, let us settle the terms. This is not jargon: it is what you need to talk to a supplier without being sold smoke, and to point the budget at the right thing.

Virtual reality (VR)

VR replaces what the traveller sees. They put on a headset, or look through a phone in 360° mode, and find themselves inside your tour: the excavations at Pompeii, the summit of Vesuvius, the alleys of Procida. It is isolated, total, immersive. It works beautifully as a pre-sale tool because it answers the international traveller's main objection: "will it really be as they say?"

Augmented reality (AR)

AR adds digital elements to the real world. The traveller points their phone at a Roman forum and sees it on screen as it was 2,000 years ago, with statues, intact columns and animated merchants. You are not replacing reality, you are enriching it. For a tour operator it is the simplest way to justify a premium ticket: the perceived experience scales while the marginal cost stays flat.

Mixed reality, for completeness

You increasingly hear about MR (Apple Vision Pro, Meta Quest 3) — a hybrid where virtual objects interact with the real environment. For now it is a niche market, a few hundred thousand headsets in Italy, so give it no more than a coffee's worth of attention until device prices fall below €500.

📌 In two lines: VR works before the booking, letting people try the tour from home. AR works during the tour, raising perceived value and justifying a higher price. Two different levers, at two different points in the funnel.

The four use cases that actually work

Ignore the noise about the "tourism metaverse". These are the four applications generating measurable bookings today.

1. Previewing the tour from home (360° VR)

From the sofa, the traveller puts on a Meta Quest headset or simply opens the 360° video on your site from their phone. They see, first-hand, the fifteen key minutes of the tour: entering the archaeological park, the guide explaining, the panoramic view at the end. They are not reading a description: they are having a sensory preview.

Measured effect on Italian tour operators that added VR previews in 2024–2025:

  • Average time on the product page: from 1:20 to 4:35
  • Conversion rate: +38–42%
  • Cart abandonment: −22%
  • Refunds after the tour: −19%, because the traveller already knows what to expect and avoids a bad impulse purchase

2. AR during the tour (historical overlay)

Walking among the ruins, the traveller opens an app or scans a QR code. The screen shows a 3D reconstruction of what they are looking at, with the guide's audio, explanatory pop-ups and animated historical figures. Missing statues reappear. Walls return to their original painted splendour. The noise of the forum, the footsteps of senators, the market traders.

The commercial consequence: you can sell the same tour as a premium experience at 30–50% above the base ticket. Pure margin, because the marginal cost per traveller is only software. And it produces a second, quieter advantage: a traveller who has had an AR experience is 2.4 times more likely to leave a positive review with photographs (TripAdvisor, 2025), which in turn drives new conversions at no cost.

3. Walking around locations (immersive street view)

Virtual tours in the manner of Google Street View, but yours and built to sell. The traveller can walk through the village, turn the view, click points of interest that open as product cards. It works very well for:

  • City tours — underground Naples, the Spanish Quarter, the historic centre
  • Visits to wineries, farms and agriturismi
  • Previews of cruises and boat trips, showing the interiors

4. Previews that drive upselling

The traveller is about to book the standard Vesuvius tour at €45. You show them, in VR, the difference between the basic entry — a group of 30, a 40-minute wait — and the "skip the line plus sunset" option at €78. Seeing the difference, 31% upgrade. Without the preview, only 7% do.

The principle works because VR makes tangible a difference that stays abstract in text or a photograph. "Sunset over the crater" is words; thirty seconds of panoramic view in warm light is a feeling. The second one sells. The first is skipped.

How to embed it on your site

Three ways, from simplest to most sophisticated:

1. An HTML5 iframe (five minutes)

Klapty, ThingLink and Matterport give you a standard HTML snippet. You paste it into the tour's product page exactly as you would a YouTube video. It works on any CMS.

2. Self-hosted 360° video

Upload the video to YouTube or Vimeo in 360° mode and use the native player. No cost, but you lose the advanced tracking: hotspot clicks, where people looked.

3. A native app with an AR SDK

AR during the tour needs an iOS and Android app built with ARKit or ARCore. Cost: €8,000–20,000 for the first release, plus maintenance. Only worth it if you sell 200 or more tickets a day.
