What "sustainable tourism" really means

Let us be clear from the start: sustainable tourism is not a sign with a green leaf at reception. It is a way of operating that reduces the property's impact on the environment, the local community and the regional economy — and proves it with measurable KPIs and certifications verified by an external body.

The UN World Tourism Organization defines it as "tourism that takes full account of its current and future economic, social and environmental impacts, addressing the needs of visitors, the industry, the environment and host communities".

📌 In two lines: sustainability in tourism means measuring consumption — water, energy, waste — reducing it through concrete measures, certifying the results with an outside body and reporting them transparently. Everything else is empty marketing, and today reviewers expose it within 48 hours.

The three dimensions

A serious approach rests on three pillars. Skip one and you are exposed to greenwashing accusations from better-informed travellers.

1. Environmental

The most visible: energy, water, waste, CO₂ emissions, biodiversity. It covers LED lighting, solar panels, flow restrictors, going plastic-free, proper recycling and food-waste tracking.

2. Social

This concerns workers' rights — proper contracts, training, gender equality — the relationship with the community (local suppliers, regional events, accessibility) and the host culture (traditions, language, craftsmanship).

3. Economic

Sustainability has to be financially sound: measures need a demonstrable return, the property has to stay competitive, and the revenue should circulate locally through nearby suppliers and local employment.

How Booking, Airbnb and Expedia reward certified properties

The OTAs are not environmental charities: they do this because their algorithms show that users convert better on certified properties. Here is how it works, platform by platform.

Booking.com — Travel Sustainable programme

Since 2022 Booking has awarded a "Travel Sustainable" badge at three levels, according to how many criteria you meet — from five basic ones to more than twenty advanced. An external certification such as Green Key or Travelife takes you straight to Level 3, the highest. Level 3 properties get:

  • Priority placement in the eco filters, used by 41% of European users in 2025
  • A badge visible in search results and on the property page
  • A dedicated slot in themed marketing emails
  • 18% higher click-through on average than uncertified properties

Airbnb — sustainability filters

Airbnb has introduced "renewable energy", "eco transport" and "minimal waste" filters in search. Properties that declare — with documentary evidence — that they meet them appear earlier in results and in personalised feeds. The Plus segment gives further priority to externally certified hosts.

Expedia Group — sustainable hotel filter

Expedia, Hotels.com and Vrbo share a "Sustainable" filter. To be included you need a GSTC-recognised certification — Green Key, Travelife, EU Ecolabel and EarthCheck all qualify. The filter is used by 28% of American travellers coming to Europe.

How to talk about it without sounding hollow

The measures alone are not enough: badly communicated, they become cost without extra revenue. Three practical rules we apply with clients.

1. Tell the story in numbers, not adjectives

Wrong: "We are an environmentally conscious property." Right: "We cut our energy consumption by 58% in 2025 with a 6 kWp solar array and 100% LED lighting. That is the equivalent of taking four cars off the road for a year."

2. A dedicated sustainability page

A page on your site with: the certifications you hold, with official badges; your annual KPIs, published; a list of local suppliers by name, with a map; real photographs of the work, not stock images; and the person responsible, with a name, not "our team".

3. Weave it into the stay

A bilingual card in the room with the three things a guest can do to help ("reuse your towel: you save 60 litres of water and 0.4 kWh"), a QR code to the sustainability page, and pre-stay and post-stay emails showing their personal impact.

⚠️ Greenwashing does more harm than good. Real cases: an Italian resort claimed "100% plastic-free" in 2024 while serving drinks in PET bottles — a guest posted the video, it reached the TripAdvisor front page, and reviews fell from 4.6 to 3.9 in two months. A Tuscan hotel declared itself "carbon neutral" without evidence; a journalist exposed it, fourteen negative articles followed and the offsetting partner withdrew. The golden rule: if you do not have an external certification and measurable KPIs, do not claim it. Talk about the individual measures — LED, local sourcing, green electricity — but never make a total claim.
