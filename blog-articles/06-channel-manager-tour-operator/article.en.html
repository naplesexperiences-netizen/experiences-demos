Tour operators and hotels need different tools

Hotel channel managers (see the dedicated guide) manage rooms as static units. Tour operator systems have to handle something far more complicated: experiences with moving parts.

Using a hotel channel manager for a tour business is like doing advanced maths on a pocket calculator: fine for simple sums, but it falls apart as soon as things get complicated.

What actually goes wrong without one

1. Duplicated, out-of-sync inventory

The same 9am slot shows as available on GetYourGuide, Viator, Airbnb and your own site. Two bookings arrive in the same minute on different channels. Overbooking. You have to cancel a customer who has already paid.

2. Inconsistent pricing

You raised the "Pompeii half day" tour from €45 to €52 on GetYourGuide. On Viator it is still €45. On Airbnb it is €40. Three prices for the same tour means confused customers, bad reviews and lost margin.

3. An overloaded team

Your people spend 70% of their time on data entry instead of selling, building relationships and improving the experiences. Burnout within six months, dependably.

4. No room to grow

You would like to add 20 tours. You would like to enter the US and German markets. You would like to launch a new destination. But you cannot: the current setup would collapse.

5. No usable analytics

Which tour has the best margin? Which OTA brings the highest-spending customers? What is the conversion rate on each channel? You do not know. You decide blind.

⚠️ A warning sign: if you have more than 30 active tours and still manage availability in shared spreadsheets or over WhatsApp between staff, you are already in dangerous territory. It is only a matter of time before the first big, public overbooking.

What a tour operator channel manager needs in 2026

1. Multi-level inventory management

The system has to handle hierarchical inventory:

  • Physical resources: one coach is 50 seats, one boat is 12
  • People: which guides are available, for which slot and which language
  • External resources: museum tickets, pre-booked entries
  • Restrictions: minimum and maximum participants, age, language

2. Automatic dynamic pricing

Rules that change the price according to:

  • How far ahead of the tour date the booking is
  • How full the slot already is
  • The season
  • Local events and holidays
  • Historical demand, with machine learning

3. Two-way APIs with every OTA

Native, certified connections — not scraping — with:

  • GetYourGuide (official API)
  • Viator (Bókun API)
  • Airbnb Experiences (selective)
  • Musement
  • Klook
  • Civitatis
  • Headout
  • Tiqets

4. A booking engine for your own site

The system should include or integrate with a proper booking engine for your site, offering:

  • Multiple languages and currencies
  • Online payment (Stripe, PayPal, Klarna)
  • A mobile-first experience
  • Automatic cross-selling

5. Operations

  • Automatic manifests for guides, with the participant list
  • Automatic pre-tour messages to customers
  • Cancellations and refunds with differentiated rules
  • Mobile check-in lists

6. Reporting and business intelligence

  • Margin by tour, by OTA, by season
  • Conversion rate per channel
  • Customer lifetime value by source
  • Demand forecasting

Dynamic pricing for tours, and what it returns

Dynamic pricing means changing the price automatically according to demand, lead time and occupancy. For tours it works even better than for hotels, because there are more moving variables.

A real example: the Pompeii half-day tour

SituationStatic priceDynamic price
60 days out, no bookings€48€42 (−12%, to encourage early booking)
21 days out, 60% full€48€48 (standard)
7 days out, 85% full€48€56 (+17%, scarcity)
2 days out, 95% full€48€64 (+33%, last seats)
Same day, one seat left€48€72 (+50%, impulse)

Industry research shows dynamic pricing produces on average 15–25% more RevPAX (revenue per available passenger) than static pricing, without reducing volume.

The five layers of dynamic pricing

  1. Seasonal (the base): different prices for high, mid and low season
  2. Weekend against weekday: a premium on Saturdays and Sundays
  3. Events: a premium for holidays, long weekends and local events
  4. Lead time: early bird, standard, last minute
  5. Occupancy: automatic increases as thresholds are reached

Modern systems let you stack all five at once, with rules that combine automatically.
