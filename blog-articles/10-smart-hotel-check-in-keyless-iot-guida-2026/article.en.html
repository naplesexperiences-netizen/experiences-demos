What a smart hotel really is

Let us clear away the cliché: a smart hotel is not a hotel full of robots. It is a property where the low-value processes are automated and the high-value ones — the relationship with the guest, solving problems, the experience — are strengthened by technology. There are four operational dimensions.

1. Check-in and check-out

Online pre-check-in, document upload, digital signature, payment, smart key delivery. Average time spent at the property: from 11 minutes to 90 seconds.

2. The in-room experience

Smart thermostat, adaptive lighting, voice assistant, a smart TV the guest can log into with their own Netflix account, a control tablet, personalised scenes: relax, work, sleep.

3. Energy and operations

Presence sensors that switch off climate control and lights, window sensors that stop the air conditioning, a central energy dashboard, predictive maintenance.

4. Data and personalisation

A unified CRM, segmentation, automatic messaging before, during and after the stay, intelligent upselling, dynamic pricing based on real demand.

📌 In two lines: a smart hotel is not technology in every corner. It is invisible technology that removes friction for the guest and cost for the property. If the guest notices it, it is probably badly designed.

The most common mistakes

Across four years of smart hotel projects, these five repeat with worrying regularity. Knowing them in advance saves months.

1. Buying hardware before choosing the software

The classic: the owner orders 60 smart locks because they were on offer, then discovers they do not integrate with the chosen platform. The result: hardware to resell and four months lost. The rule: choose the platform first, then the hardware the vendor certifies.

2. Underestimating the network

Smart locks, thermostats, sensors and TVs need stable Wi-Fi, segmented so guest traffic and IoT traffic are on separate networks, with coverage in every corner. An investment of €3,000–8,000 in professional access points is almost always necessary in older Italian buildings.

3. No fallback for the guest who cannot or will not

There will always be guests who cannot or do not want to use digital check-in. You need a clear alternative: a person available, a valid printed code in an envelope, an emergency number. Without one, a frustrated guest leaves a one-star review.

4. Cutting staff instead of redeploying them

A smart hotel does not reduce people, it reduces low-value tasks. Properties that made their reception redundant saw NPS fall by 12 to 18 points. Properties that redeployed those roles as guest experience hosts saw it rise by 20 to 25.

5. Not measuring before launch

Without a baseline — current check-in time, current NPS, current energy costs — you cannot show the return. Measure for at least 30 days before starting. That data is what will convince you to carry on through the inevitable hiccups of the first month.

Privacy and GDPR

A smart hotel collects sensitive data: documents, optionally biometrics, behaviour. Compliance is not optional.

  • Facial recognition: only on a strong legal basis with explicit consent; it must be optional and never the only way in
  • Biometric data: a special category under article 9 GDPR, requiring a data protection impact assessment
  • Document retention: a maximum of 30 days for the copy; beyond that, only the metadata required by the authorities
  • Encryption: required both in transit and at rest for identity data
  • A multilingual privacy notice: clear, available before check-in, with non-digital alternatives offered
  • Vendor due diligence: make sure the platform is GDPR-compliant with servers in the EU
⚠️ Smart does not mean cold. The biggest risk of a smart hotel is feeling dehumanised. The golden rule: automate what the guest does not want to do, not what they enjoy doing. Check-in is in the first group — nobody loves a queue. A restaurant recommendation is in the second. Keep a human host for the welcome, problem solving and relationships, ideally redeployed from reception rather than removed. Properties that eliminated every human presence saw NPS and reviews collapse within twelve months.

Implementation roadmap: six months to a complete system

Month 1 — Audit and platform choice

  1. Map the existing check-in and check-out processes
  2. Audit the network: Wi-Fi in rooms, coverage
  3. Shortlist three platforms and take demos
  4. Check integration with your existing PMS
  5. Choose the vendor and sign

Month 2 — Software setup

  1. Configure the check-in platform
  2. Integrate the PMS and the guest reporting system
  3. Set up the pre-arrival email and SMS flow
  4. Test with internal bookings

Months 3–4 — In-room hardware

  1. Install smart locks, testing on five pilot rooms first
  2. Smart thermostats and presence sensors
  3. Window sensors
  4. Smart TVs, if planned
  5. Roll out across every room

Month 5 — Training and the new operating model

  1. Train the team on the new flow
  2. Redeploy reception roles into guest experience
  3. Backup procedures: what to do when a guest cannot manage the technology
  4. Soft launch on direct bookings

Month 6 — Launch and tuning

  1. Launch across every channel
  2. Weekly KPI monitoring
  3. Tune on guest feedback
  4. Communicate it on the OTAs and your site with a "smart check-in" badge
